The tree that remembers: why what you just used should be the fastest thing to find

Data Structures and Algorithms

The song you replay. The chat you open twenty times a day. The same three files, all week. Most of what you ask a computer for, you asked for a moment ago.

A search tree can ignore that and treat every item alike, or it can bet on it. So the question is: should a data structure reorganize itself around what you use the most?

The bet: you will ask again

  • Inside Python. The standard library can remember the most recent calls of a function. Its documentation says when that pays off: when "the most recent calls are the best predictors of upcoming calls", like the most popular articles on a news server.[1]
  • Inside BSD systems. Their tree library includes the splay tree, a search tree that moves every item you look up to the top. The manual gives the reason: requests that repeat find their items near the top, so lookups get faster.[2]
Use a self-adjusting tree when a few items get most of the requests.

The price: no promise for a single request

  • Inside Windows. Driver code can keep its data in "generic tables", built on splay trees by default. The same page warns that a splay tree can become deep and narrow, even a straight line, and offers a strictly balanced tree as the alternative.[3]
  • Reading is writing. A splay tree rearranges itself on every lookup, so even a search changes memory.[2] What it guarantees is the total: any M operations in a row, starting from an empty tree, take at most about M · log N steps, although one of them may be slow.[4]
A splay tree is fast over many operations, not on each one.

Did you know?

Splay trees were introduced in 1985 by Daniel Sleator and Robert Tarjan, in a paper called "Self-adjusting binary search trees". The name says it all: the tree adjusts itself to the way you use it.[5]

Think about it

  1. Think of the apps on your phone. Do a few of them get most of your taps? Where would you want those to sit in a tree?
  2. A splay tree may answer one request slowly and the next thousand quickly. Name one program where that is fine, and one where it is not.
  3. If a search also rearranges the tree, what could go wrong when two parts of a program search it at the same moment?
